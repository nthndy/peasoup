It's been a while since I updated this blog, as I've been away for a month. I was a bit nervous about leaving the pond, considering how closely I'd been monitoring it in the preceding weeks, but gardens take time and patience and all those good things, so I figured this was a good opportunity to really test the pond's self-sustaining credentials as we move into autumn.

![A wide view of the pond, with the security camera on the rim, lilies and mare's tail in the water, and purple verbena blooming along the stone edge]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/everything_4oct.jpg)

Snails. The first thing I noticed were snails, absolutely everywhere — climbing up the central water column, milling about the outer walls, tucked into the corners of all the plant pots. An excellent and encouraging sign of biodiversity.

![Snails spread across the pond's walls and plant pots]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/snails_4oct.gif)

Peering a bit deeper in, which is all the easier now the (somewhat) cooler autumn weather has clarified the algal bloom, I was super chuffed to see a veritable hive of underwater energy, with tiny little shrimps and crustaceans milling about like it's rush hour in my own little Atlantis.

![Tiny shrimp-like crustaceans swarming through the clearer autumn water]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/shrimp_city_4oct.gif)

It's difficult to identify all these creatures, as they're right at the resolution limit of both my phone camera and my own eyes. Perhaps I need to get a microscope — more on that at a later date. My informed guess is that they're likely a mix of seed shrimp, daphnia and copepods. They play a crucial role in the pond ecosystem that's now established itself, acting as a near-invisible army of tiny composters, gobbling up decaying plant material and excreting it as a steady drip of ammonia waste that then, in [turn](https://youtu.be/W3xgcmIS3YU), feeds the beneficial bacteria which, in [turn](https://youtu.be/W3xgcmIS3YU), convert that into nitrites, which then, in [turn](https://youtu.be/W3xgcmIS3YU), get [turned](https://youtu.be/W3xgcmIS3YU) into nitrates that act, in [turn](https://youtu.be/W3xgcmIS3YU), as fertiliser for all the resident pond plants. It is, quite literally, the [circle of life](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dQw4w9WgXcQ).

One creature I am one hundred percent certain of the identification on: DRAGONFLY NYMPHS. I started pulling the plants out of the water to top up some soil that had spilled, courtesy of a vandal magpie, caught in the act below.

![A magpie about to tip soil out of one of the plant pots]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/magpie_prevandalism_27sept.gif)

![The same pot, soil spilled, after the magpie moved on]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/magpie_postvandalism_27sept.gif)

Putting everything back in, I quickly realised that pretty much every single plant was teeming with these slightly monstrous, earwig-looking little things.

![Several mature dragonfly nymphs clinging to the submerged roots]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/mature_dragonfly_nymphs_4oct.jpg)

![A dragonfly nymph up close among the roots]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/dragonfly_nymp_4oct.jpg)

Building on our picture of the delicate pond nitrogen cycle — we've already met those sweet, innocent little shrimpies milling about the side of the pond — these dragonfly nymphs sit at the polar opposite end of the threat spectrum. Even in their immature nymph stage, they're the absolute apex predators of this ecosystem. They lie in ambush in the vegetation, filling a rectal chamber with water before violently contracting it, forcefully jetting that water back out and rocketing themselves forward like tiny ass-jet-propelled dinosaurs. They also have horrifyingly Alien-esque (as in the movie) labial "jaws" that shoot out from their face in a slingshot strike to snatch up any unwitting prey. I couldn't quite get this spring-loaded face trap on camera, but I did catch one absolutely yeeting away after I flicked it back into the water. Fly, my pretties.

![A dragonfly nymph jet-propelling itself away through the water]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/swimming_dragonfly_nymph_4oct.gif)

Some species can take years to mature underwater — up to five for the largest UK dragonflies — moulting their exoskeleton more than a dozen times along the way. This maturation is an incredibly energy-intensive process, and the relative temperature stability of the pond means they can safely survive winter in the silty submarine mud while feasting on a veritable smorgasbord of other pondlife. Importantly, that diet includes mosquito larvae when they're present, so having so many dragonfly nymphs around is a great sign that a population of mosquitoes won't be able to establish itself in this pond. After realising that the next generation of dragonflies across central London depended entirely on me keeping these pots submerged, I had to take stock of the plants while they were still in the water. I'll run through them quickly in order now.

Firstly, the [mare's tail]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-mare-s-tail) (*Hippuris vulgaris*) is thriving — these are simply the best-adapted plants to this particular pond setup. All three of the new pots are doing very well, so I moved them to a few different spots, including two on the fountain shelf, to see whether the roots and fronds will grow down the water cascade and soften/green up the appearance of the central column a bit. That would be a dream outcome — tumbling plants everywhere. I did something similar with the older mare's tail too, bringing its extensive root and frond network up and draping it over the outer wall of the pond, mainly so it doesn't strangle the other plants. Two of the new rushes I planted out — the [white flowering rush]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-white-flowering-rush) (*Butomus umbellatus* 'Alba') and the [hard rush]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-hard-rush) (*Juncus inflexus*) — are struggling a bit, but mainly because their pots fell off the stone shelf I built for them, spilling some of the soil. I'm surprised they're still alive after clinging on to whatever little substrate they had left for a month; I've topped them up and set them right. The other, more established rush on the left of this picture — the original flowering rush — is thriving, so I expect the other two will catch up now as well. The new [fibre optic plant]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-fibre-optic-plant) (*Scirpus cernuus*) had a similarly orthogonal September, but seems to be thriving nonetheless, so I moved one onto the fountain shelf to see if it thrives there too. Finally for this picture, the [floating heart]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-floating-heart) (*Nymphoides peltata*) is having a bit of a strugglesome time, with most of its adult leaves having dropped off and swum away, but there are still green shoots coming from the base, so this one's probably just entering a winter dormancy state. We've all been there.

![Mare's tail, rushes, the fibre optic plant and the floating heart together on the fountain shelf]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/mares_rush_fibre_peltata_4oct.jpg)

Next, the lilies are mostly doing well — the most mature one, on the right here, the original [Alba lily]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-alba-lily) (*Nymphaea alba*), still has a healthy canopy of differently aged pads. The other lilies, the younger [unnamed water lilies]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-unnamed-water-lily) (*Nymphaea* sp.), have had quite a bit of pad die-back, but still have healthy crowns, and one even put out another flower while I was away. Several of the pads look as if they've been skeletonised, which I've learned is likely a mix of natural dieback and the soft tissue rotting away to leave the vein skeleton behind, as the plant pulls energy back into its rhizome for the colder months. Much the same can be said for the [water knotweed]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-willow-grass) (*Persicaria amphibia*), which has lost most of its leaf cover and looks a bit straggly, but still has a healthy green crown.

![Water lilies, water knotweed and mare's tail in the main pond]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/lily_knotweed_mares_4oct.jpg)

![The Alba lily's flower, open above its canopy of pads]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/alba_lily_4oct.jpg)

The absolute hulk of the pond in its current state is the [lesser pond sedge]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-lesser-pond-sedge) (*Carex acutiformis*), thriving and towering to about a metre tall on the right of this photo. I might order a few more, as they give the pond a nice bit of structure, but I could also try cleaving a bit off and repotting it come spring. In fact, I might do that before autumn really sets in, since it's getting a little too big for its ~~boots~~ basket. The [water hawthorn]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-water-hawthorn) (*Aponogeton distachyos*) is also looking good, with a few small flowers still in place, but also showing a hint of dormancy prep. The original [flowering rush]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-flowering-rush) (*Butomus umbellatus*) — the oldest of the three — is thriving too, on the left of this photo, as is the fibre optic plant. The floating heart in this photo is going through the same pre-winter pruning as the other one. There's also a loose peltata frond that came away from its soil and is still going strong — I anchored it to some stones in the main pond and, surprisingly, it seems to like it there. Apparently too good for soil, happy just being pegged down by a rock.

![Water hawthorn, flowering rush, fibre optic plant, lesser pond sedge and lily all together]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/hawthorn_rush_fibre_carex_lily_4oct.jpg)

I've also added some more [common duckweed]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-common-duckweed) (*Lemna minor*) to encourage a bit more greenery, as shown here.

![Water hawthorn with a fresh scattering of duckweed]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/hawthorn_duckweed_4oct.jpg)

I'm surprised by how well the shelf plants have done, given they were only middling at best when I left. The [marsh marigold]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-marsh-marigold) (*Caltha palustris*) loves it up here, so I'll look to get more of those in future. The [water mint]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-water-mint) (*Mentha aquatica*) is also thriving on this shelf and producing a wonderful minty fragrance — imagine if I could fill the whole shelf with it, tumbling down the edges too. The [water forget-me-not]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-water-forget-me-not) (*Myosotis scorpioides*) is doing just fine here too, so I added what might be a dormant pot of the same species from my balcony pond alongside it. Funnily enough, that potted plant died back after thriving all summer, but it's since seeded and spawned another forget-me-not in a side crack of the balcony pond — they seem hardier than I first thought, and probably prefer somewhat drier conditions than I'd assumed. Finally, the [brooklime]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-brooklime) (*Veronica beccabunga*) and [creeping jenny]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-creeping-jenny) (*Lysimachia nummularia* 'Aurea') both seem to be struggling a little, even on the water shelf — they're about half the size they used to be, though still green and seemingly healthy. The [hornwort]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-hornwort) (*Ceratophyllum demersum*), [willow moss]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-willow-moss) (*Fontinalis antipyretica*) and [water starwort]({{ site.baseurl }}/plants/#plant-water-starwort) (*Callitriche*) have all seemingly gone to the great pond in the sky (dead and digested).

![Brooklime, water forget-me-not, marsh marigold and water mint on the upper shelf]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/img/beccabunga_forgetmenot_marshmarigold_watermint_4oct.jpg)

Here's today's water test — pretty similar to usual at this point: pH of 8–9, ammonia at 0, nitrite somewhere between 0 and 0.25, and phosphate at around 7.5.
